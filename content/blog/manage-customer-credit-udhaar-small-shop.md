Almost every neighbourhood shop in India runs on trust. A regular customer forgets their wallet, a family settles the month’s groceries after payday, a small restaurant takes supplies on credit and pays on Saturday. That trust, recorded as **udhaar** in a khata, is part of why customers keep coming back.

It is also one of the easiest ways for a shop to run short of cash. Money that customers owe is money you cannot use to restock the shelves. This guide sets out a simple system for giving credit that keeps customers happy and keeps your cash moving.

## Why customer credit goes wrong

Credit rarely fails because customers are dishonest. It usually fails because of small gaps in the system:

- **Entries are missed.** A busy evening, a quick “write it later”, and an amount never reaches the book.
- **Totals are unclear.** Customers are shown a long list of scribbled entries instead of a clean balance.
- **There is no limit.** A small balance slowly becomes a large one because nobody decided where to stop.
- **Reminders feel awkward.** The owner avoids asking, so old balances stay open for months.

Each of these is fixable with a few habits.

## Decide who gets credit

Not every customer needs the same terms. A short written rule helps you stay consistent and makes it easier to say no politely.

| Customer type | Suggested approach |
|---|---|
| New or occasional customer | Cash or instant payment only |
| Regular customer for a few months | Small credit, settled within a week |
| Long-standing family or business customer | Higher limit, settled on an agreed day each month |

When you explain that “this is how we do it for everyone”, customers are far less likely to take a refusal personally.

## Set a limit for every customer

A credit limit is the maximum balance a customer can carry. A sensible starting point is roughly what that customer buys in one or two weeks. Two questions help you decide:

1. If this balance were never paid, could the shop still buy its next stock order?
2. Has this customer settled on time before?

Raise the limit only after a customer has paid on time a few times. Lower it if payments start slipping. Writing the limit next to the customer’s name in your khata makes it visible every time you add an entry.

## Record every entry the same way

A credit record is only useful if it is complete and consistent. Every entry should include:

- the **date**
- **what was bought**, even briefly (“rice 5 kg, oil 1 L”)
- the **amount**
- the **running balance** after the entry

Payments should be recorded the same way, as a dated line that reduces the balance. If more than one person works at the counter, agree that whoever gives credit writes the entry immediately, before serving the next customer.

> **Tip:** At the end of each day, check the total credit given against your bills or sales slips. A missing entry is much easier to find on the same evening than at the end of the month.

## Agree on when credit will be settled

Ambiguity is the biggest enemy of collections. “Pay when you can” almost always becomes “pay much later”. Agree on a simple, specific settlement time:

- weekly customers settle on the same day each week
- monthly customers settle in the first week of the month
- business customers settle on a fixed date that suits their own cash cycle

Write the agreed day in the ledger. It turns a reminder from a request into a routine.

## Ask for payment without hurting the relationship

Most customers intend to pay. A reminder simply helps them remember. A good reminder is:

- **specific**: the exact amount and the dates of the purchases
- **private**: never in front of other customers
- **easy to act on**: tell them how they can pay right away

A short message works well: *“Namaste, your balance with us is the amount shown below for purchases from the 3rd to the 18th. You can pay at the counter or by UPI whenever convenient this week. Thank you!”*

If a balance keeps growing, pause new credit politely until the old amount is settled. Being consistent protects both the shop and the relationship.

## Review your credit book every week

Once a week, spend fifteen minutes on four questions:

1. What is the total amount customers owe the shop today?
2. Which balances are older than the agreed settlement time?
3. Which customers are close to or above their limit?
4. Is the total credit growing faster than sales?

If the total keeps rising week after week, the shop is funding its customers with its own cash. That is the moment to tighten limits or follow up more firmly.

## When a notebook is no longer enough

A paper khata can work well for a few dozen customers. As the shop grows, the same problems appear more often: balances added up wrong, pages damaged, and no quick way to see who has been overdue the longest. A digital ledger solves most of these because it totals balances automatically and keeps the history of every entry.

## A simple checklist

- Decide in writing who can get credit and on what terms.
- Set a limit for every credit customer.
- Record every entry and payment with a date and a running balance.
- Agree on a settlement day and write it down.
- Send specific, private and friendly reminders.
- Review the whole credit book once a week.

Credit handled this way stays what it was meant to be: a sign of trust that brings customers back, not a quiet drain on your cash.
