Most small shops know their best-selling items by heart. Where things slip is everywhere else: the item that quietly ran out on a busy weekend, the box of goods that expired at the back of the shelf, the supplier order placed twice because nobody was sure what was in the stockroom.

Good stock tracking does not need to be complicated. It needs a routine that someone actually follows. This guide gives you one you can start this week.

## Why stock tracking matters

Stock is usually the largest amount of money a small shop has tied up at any moment. When it is not tracked, three problems show up:

- **Stockouts.** Customers ask for an item you have run out of and buy it elsewhere, sometimes for good.
- **Overstock.** Cash sits on the shelf in slow-moving goods instead of paying for items that sell.
- **Shrinkage.** Goods disappear through damage, expiry, billing errors or theft, and nobody notices until the month’s numbers look wrong.

A simple routine catches all three early.

## Sort your items into three groups

Not every item deserves the same attention. Split your stock into groups based on how much they matter to sales:

| Group | What goes in it | How closely to track |
|---|---|---|
| A: fast movers | The items that bring in most of your sales | Count every week |
| B: steady sellers | Regular items that sell at a predictable pace | Count every month |
| C: slow movers | Occasional purchases, seasonal goods | Check every quarter |

In most shops a small number of items brings in most of the sales. Focusing your weekly effort there gives you the biggest return for the least time.

## The weekly routine

Pick a quiet hour on the same day each week. Then:

1. **Count your A items.** Physically count what is on the shelf and in the stockroom.
2. **Compare with your records.** Note any item where the count differs from what you expected.
3. **Check reorder levels.** Mark every item that has fallen to or below its reorder level.
4. **Look at expiry dates.** Move items that expire soon to the front and consider a small discount.
5. **Prepare the purchase list.** Turn the marked items into a supplier order.

The whole routine usually takes under an hour once it becomes a habit, and it replaces a lot of guesswork.

## How to set a reorder level

A reorder level tells you when to buy more of an item before you run out. You need two numbers:

- **Average daily sales**: how many units you usually sell per day.
- **Lead time**: how many days your supplier usually takes to deliver.

Multiply the two, then add a small safety buffer for busy days or late deliveries:

> **Reorder level = average daily sales × lead time + safety buffer**

For example, if you sell about 6 bags of rice a day and your supplier delivers in 3 days, you need about 18 bags to cover the wait. Adding a buffer of 6 bags gives a reorder level of 24. When the shelf and stockroom together hold 24 bags, it is time to order.

Review reorder levels every few months. Sales patterns change with seasons, festivals and new competitors nearby.

## Find and reduce shrinkage

Shrinkage is the gap between what your records say you should have and what you actually have. Some gap is normal. A gap that keeps growing for the same items is a signal. Check the causes in this order, because the first ones are the most common:

1. **Billing mistakes**: items sold but not billed, or billed under the wrong name.
2. **Receiving mistakes**: deliveries accepted without counting against the supplier’s bill.
3. **Damage and expiry**: goods thrown away without being written off in the records.
4. **Theft**: from outside or inside the shop.

Counting deliveries when they arrive, and writing off damaged goods the same day, fixes a surprising share of shrinkage.

## Keep stock and billing connected

The weakness of most manual systems is that stock and sales live in different places. The bill says two bags were sold; the stock register only knows if someone remembers to update it. Every gap between the two creates a mismatch you have to investigate later.

## Your stock-tracking checklist

- Group your items into fast movers, steady sellers and slow movers.
- Count fast movers every week on the same day.
- Set a reorder level for every important item and review it every few months.
- Count every delivery against the supplier’s bill before accepting it.
- Write off damaged and expired goods the day you find them.
- Investigate any item whose count is repeatedly lower than expected.

Stock tracking is less about the tool and more about the rhythm. A shop that counts the right items on the right day rarely runs out of what customers want, and rarely pays for goods it does not need.
